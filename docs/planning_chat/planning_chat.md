## 4. Claude (2026-10-15T04:40:56.912691Z)

The treatment assumed a 1950s jingle, but the concept fits several styles. The best ones copy the sound of real political advertising, because that's what the AI money is buying.

1. **Modern heartland campaign ad (country-pop).** This is what real 2026 ads sound like: strummed acoustic guitar, a swelling piano, golden-hour fields, a pickup truck. The song is a sincere small-town anthem about hard work and family, and the data center in the background slowly fills the field. Generators handle this sound very well, and because it is so close to real ads, the satire stings more than a period piece.

2. **Gilbert & Sullivan patter song.** The disclaimer becomes the whole song: a breathless list of everything the PAC supports (roads, jobs, grandma, the troops) sung faster and faster, with AI buried in the middle where nobody can catch it. The old patter tunes are public domain, so you can use the real melody. The risk is that rapid diction is where generated vocals garble. That could also be part of the joke, since disclaimers are meant to be unintelligible.

3. **1950s TV jingle (the original pitch).** A bright crooner with doo-wop backing, from the era when TV campaign jingles began. It's the most instantly readable as "political ad," and the period look puts some ironic distance between the song and real candidates.

4. **1970s educational cartoon song.** A cheerful, explanatory children's-TV number about how a super PAC works: independent expenditures, no coordination with the candidate, and money that can go to safe seats. It teaches the mechanics while staying friendly, and an animated style avoids any real faces. Keep it an original tune and look rather than echoing a specific show.

5. **Country duet.** The two rival networks sing as a feuding couple, each insisting they're nothing alike, to the same melody and the same dodges. This keeps the "both sides sound identical" joke from the treatment, and two alternating solo voices are easier for generators than close harmony.

6. **Brass-band rally march.** A Sousa-style march with a sung chorus, with bunting, balloons and a crowd chanting a slogan that never says what the product is. Sousa's marches are public domain. It's loud and fun, but it's more of a spectacle than a joke, so it needs the swerve rhymes to carry the satire.

I'd lean toward the **heartland ad** for reach and accuracy, or the **patter song** if you want the fine-print idea to be the whole video. The 1950s jingle is the safe middle option. Two styles are already taken elsewhere: the revival tent is held for season two, and the regression-to-the-mean song covers generic pop.
